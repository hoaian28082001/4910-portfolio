Senior Design · Project 18 · EE 4910 / 4920

Eddy Current Probe for Conductivity Measurement

Fall 2026 – Spring 2027 · Team of 6 · Faculty advisor: Dr. Jiming Song

Description

Our six-member team is developing an eddy current probe to measure the electrical conductivity of metals without damaging the sample. An alternating current in the probe's coil produces a changing magnetic field that induces eddy currents in a nearby metal. These currents affect the coil's electrical impedance. By measuring this response and comparing it with reference samples, the system aims to estimate the metal's conductivity.

My role

I help coordinate team activities, organize project tasks, and communicate with our faculty advisor. I also contribute to researching the measurement method, developing circuit diagrams and calculations, and planning prototype construction and laboratory testing. My electrical engineering background supports the team's circuit analysis and interpretation of measurement results.

Skills & knowledge gained

Through this project, I am developing my understanding of electromagnetic induction, eddy currents, and the relationship between conductivity and coil impedance. I am also strengthening my skills in circuit design, experimental planning, technical documentation, teamwork, and project coordination. As development progresses, I will gain additional experience in prototype testing, calibration, and evaluating measurement accuracy.

Course Projects

Project I · EE 230 · Electronic Circuits & Systems

Temperature Indicator Circuit

Spring 2025 · Team of 3

Description

Our three-person team designed, simulated, and built a temperature indicator circuit using operational amplifiers configured as comparators and three LEDs. The circuit was designed for an LM35 temperature sensor, which produces a voltage proportional to temperature. During testing, we used a variable DC voltage source to simulate the sensor output.

As temperature increased, the green LED turned off and the orange LED turned on at approximately 40 °C. Above 100 °C, the red LED also turned on. During cooling, the red LED remained on until the temperature dropped below 80 °C, while the orange LED remained on until below 30 °C, when the green LED turned back on. These different switching thresholds provided hysteresis, helping prevent rapid switching near a temperature boundary.

My role

I worked with my teammates to develop the circuit design, determine resistor values and reference voltages, and verify the LED switching behavior. I contributed to circuit simulation, breadboard construction, testing, and documenting our design. Our team also resolved a reference-voltage loading issue that occurred when the three comparator circuits were connected together by adjusting the voltage-divider resistors.

Skills & knowledge gained

This project strengthened my understanding of inverting and non-inverting comparators, positive feedback, and hysteresis. I gained experience translating design requirements into voltage thresholds, selecting resistor values, using LTspice to evaluate circuit behavior, and testing a physical prototype. Troubleshooting the reference-voltage issue also helped me understand how connecting circuit stages can affect their operation.

Resources used

We used LTspice for schematic development and simulation, a breadboard and EE 230 lab-kit components for construction, and DC power supplies for powering and testing the circuit. Gary Tuttle's comparator lecture notes and Excel calculation spreadsheet supported our threshold calculations and resistor selection. We also used the course's temperature indicator design requirements to guide the circuit's behavior and testing.

Project II · EE 3030 · Energy Systems & Power Electronics

Power Electronics and Three-Phase Energy System Simulation

Fall 2025 · PLECS

Description

This project involved implementing and analyzing a power electronics system in PLECS. The model connected a DC source and boost converter to a three-phase inverter, an LC filter, transformers, a transmission line, and multiple loads. The boost converter regulated the DC bus around a nominal 1,500 V, while a downstream buck converter supplied a nominal 48 V DC load. I examined voltage, current, and average-power waveforms at several locations to evaluate how the system converted and delivered electrical energy.

My role

I implemented the boost converter and three power electronics load subsystems within the course model, configured converter controls, and ran simulations to evaluate their operation. I collected and interpreted DC-bus voltage, buck-converter output, three-phase voltage and current, and average-power traces. I documented the circuit configurations and simulation results in my final report.

Skills & knowledge gained

This project strengthened my understanding of boost and buck converters, three-phase inverters, pulse-width modulation, rectification, and filtering. I gained experience using PLECS to model interconnected power systems and evaluate voltage regulation, switching ripple, and transient responses. Comparing waveforms throughout the model helped me connect individual converter behavior with the operation of the overall energy system.

Resources used

I used PLECS to implement the circuit, configure control blocks, and simulate system behavior. Resources included the course-provided model and assignment instructions, EE 3030 course material, and simulation scopes for recording voltage, current, and power. The model incorporated switching devices, inductors, capacitors, transformers, transmission-line elements, and measurement blocks.

Project III · EE 330 · Integrated Circuits

Digital Potentiometer and Programmable Amplifier Integrated Circuit

Spring 2026 · Team of 2 · Cadence Virtuoso

Description

Our two-person team designed and simulated a multifunctional integrated circuit in Cadence Virtuoso. A two-bit mode selector configured the circuit as a programmable non-inverting amplifier, programmable inverting amplifier, digital potentiometer, or four-bit digital-to-analog converter (DAC). The design combined a 16-tap resistor string, digital decoders, CMOS bidirectional switches, and a two-stage operational amplifier. Four-bit control inputs selected the potentiometer tap or amplifier gain. We completed the schematic and physical layout, verified the operating modes through transient simulations, and obtained clean final design-rule and layout-versus-schematic checks.

My role

I worked with my teammate to develop and integrate the circuit's analog and digital building blocks. I contributed to schematic design, simulation testbenches, physical layout, and verification. Our work included testing decoder logic and switch operation, evaluating programmable amplifier and DAC outputs, and troubleshooting control-bit ordering and amplifier stability. I also helped document the design process, results, and limitations in our final report.

Skills & knowledge gained

This project strengthened my understanding of CMOS transmission gates, digital decoders, resistor-string potentiometers, programmable amplifier feedback, and digital-to-analog conversion. I gained practical experience with hierarchical circuit design, transistor sizing, transient analysis, and integrated-circuit layout. I also learned how design-rule checking (DRC) and layout-versus-schematic (LVS) verification support physical design accuracy. Troubleshooting reinforced the importance of testing operational amplifiers in unity-gain configurations and evaluating how stabilization choices affect output accuracy and usable range.

Resources used

We used Cadence Virtuoso for schematic capture and layout, ADE Maestro for simulation, and Pegasus for DRC and LVS verification. The design used the gpdk045 process and gsclib045 logic cells. Supporting resources included EE 330 lectures and laboratory examples, the project specifications, a provided two-stage operational-amplifier design guide, transistor-parameter spreadsheets, and guidance from our instructor and teaching assistants.

Cumulative Reflection

A Reflective Journey: Navigating Your Cumulative Experience at Iowa State University

Reflecting on my years as a student at Iowa State University, I can see that my education in electrical engineering has been more than just coursework and a degree. At the time of starting my university life, I viewed the role of an engineer as a person who has to solve technical problems using math and sciences. In pursuing my major in Electrical Engineering, I realized that being an engineer involves much more than that. An engineer is supposed to solve complex problems, communicate with people from various fields, be a good team player, consider the impacts of his decisions on other people, and constantly learn because of advancements in technology.

The first major shift for me is that engineering problems should be considered from the perspective of a bigger picture. In college, it is often believed that there is only one right answer. In practice, however, things are different. It is through my electrical engineering classes that I have come to understand that just because something works does not mean that it is a good solution. Engineers need to take into consideration such aspects as cost, reliability, safety, efficiency, ecological impacts, and even the people that would benefit from the engineering project. The importance of it became clear as I started to study more and more about power systems. For instance, when studying power system analysis and economics of electric power planning, I start thinking not only about the behavior of the system electrically but also about the economics of its operation. Generating and transmitting electricity is related to utilities, their customers, communities, and even the environment.

In the course of my college education, I have come to understand a lot more about engineering problem solving. Initially, my approach would be to find the relevant equation and try to solve it. However, with increased difficulty of class problems, this became inadequate. This necessitated that I first gain an understanding of the system, know the available data, identify the objective, and then select the appropriate solution approach. Each class provided its own challenge. The circuit courses taught me how to analyze the electrical behavior using fundamental principles, Signals and Systems made me think in mathematical domains such as time, frequency, and Laplace domains, and the integrated circuit design presented yet another form of problem whereby the selection made at the transistor level impacts the circuit as a whole. These were all aimed at encouraging power thinking which is necessary to deal with complex systems in power courses.

Project work has been particularly helpful in making this transition. For instance, I created a digital potentiometer design on Cadence Virtuoso. To do so, it wasn’t enough for me to know how particular transistors behave individually. It required me to understand how all the components such as switches, resistor chains, and a decoder would work as a whole system. While comparing various designs, I realized how one was more accurate, but the other was more stable. There is also another experience when my team created a robot that would use sensors and its programmed behaviors to detect and engage with any object. It made me realize that the initial idea doesn’t always have to be the result.

This particular lesson holds even greater value to me through my Senior Design work. The team I am working with designs an eddy current probe that will measure conductivity. It is not like most homework assignments when there is a page in the textbook that guides us on how to construct a particular device. We need to study the basic theories, design the circuit, calculate the measurements, find the components, perform experiments in the lab, and modify our design based on the results obtained. Even during the preliminary discussion for our project, it was apparent how critical it is for us to have members on the team who bring different expertise in order to examine the problem from different perspectives. Some of our team members understand the concepts of integrated circuitry, RF concepts, material science, and power systems.

Another aspect of development is acquiring information, not just in the classroom environment. I realized at college that lectures cannot provide engineers with all necessary information. I used textbooks, component manuals, technical literature, software, peers, professors, and experts for technical assignments and tasks. The experience gained during my internship at Electrical Power Products (EP2) confirmed this point. As an intern in the Quality Engineering department, I encountered equipment and procedures that I was not familiar with. It included instruments such as Omicron CMC 356, protective relays, testing equipment, and electrical tests themselves. It was impossible to get all necessary information only through lectures. I had to learn everything by analyzing existing documents, observing other testers, and asking questions.

As one of my main responsibilities in the internship, I was asked to write and enhance the existing work instructions for manufacturing and testing processes. The experience taught me something important about engineering communication. Prior to the internship, I was mainly viewing engineering communication as writing a report or presenting a project. At EP², I realized that poor technical writing could have a direct influence on the performance of a certain task. A work instruction had to be technically accurate and easily comprehensible for an operator of the procedure. After receiving feedback from testers, quality engineers, and management, I revised the instructions accordingly. This process convinced me that feedback is a natural part of engineering, not a failure. In addition, it is confirmed that knowledge can be acquired from various people. The person who performs the same procedure daily can point out a problem that is not visible on the drawing or in the document.

The internship taught me how learning occurs outside the classroom setting as well. It allowed me to understand how engineering interacts with manufacturing, quality, testing, and business processes. It made me realize that engineers and technicians have to consider safety, repeatability, record keeping, timetables, and customer requirements in addition to functionality.

My internship, career fairs, discussions with engineers and recruiters, team assignments, and Senior Design projects all motivated me to search for opportunities outside the classroom. They also encouraged me to be more at ease in settings where I do not have all the answers. Rather than shying away from something because I don't know enough about it, I now realize what it is that I need to learn.

Now having all my current knowledge, I would approach my studies in a much different way. If I was able to take some of the time that I used on simply completing assignments and preparing for the upcoming tests to learn the reasons and significance of the topics and how they relate to the future classes and engineering practice, then it would be much more beneficial for me. I also need to develop better studying skills early and raise questions right away when I do not understand something. There were several occasions when I did not use the existing resources, trying to complete my tasks by myself, which was rather counterproductive. Now I understand that it is perfectly fine and even necessary to address my questions to professors, classmates, teaching assistants, or experienced engineers to learn properly. I also wish that I had explored all the branches of electrical engineering much earlier.

My learning process has been modified due to these experiences. When dealing with complex engineering concepts, I am now able to dissect problems into smaller parts and ensure that I understand what is behind each equation. I use comparisons, solve problems, and apply simulations or engineering software in order to better understand the phenomenon at hand. Different software, such as MATLAB, Simulink, Cadence Virtuoso, LTspice, PLECS, ADS, and many others demonstrate various ways of linking theory to the behavior of the actual system. In addition, I have realized that each subject requires a different approach. Fabrication of semiconductors cannot be compared to power system problems, and circuit design does not equal embedded systems programming. The readiness to adjust my learning process according to different disciplines makes me more adaptable as a learner.

Recently, I have become fascinated by power systems as an example of my learning pattern. Indeed, power systems combine elements of circuits, mathematics, control theory, economics, and physical infrastructure. Whenever I encounter an unknown element, I begin studying the theory behind it and continue with examples, until I am able to link equations to the actual system. After that, I study the application of the concept in practice in actual power grids. The internship was extremely helpful here since I had the opportunity not only to study the theoretical aspects of electrical systems, but to see protection and test equipment in practice as well. At the same time, my experience with VLSI and circuit design provides me with another perspective on electrical engineering, which is applicable to a completely different scale.

With my undergraduate coursework drawing to a close, I am well aware that graduation will not mean the end of my education. My knowledge of technology, power systems, semiconductor devices, design packages, standards, and engineering practices will continue to evolve during my entire career. While I intend to build on my knowledge of power systems, it is also important for me to retain the fundamentals of circuits and electronics which I have learned through my other courses. One of the things that I plan to do to develop further as an engineer is to study and prepare for the FE Electrical and Computer exam. In addition to that, I also plan to get experience with some of the common software and drawings used by engineers.

There have been many difficulties and challenges in my college experience at Iowa State University, but these challenges made my educational journey very enriching and productive. It was a difficult curriculum, challenging new concepts, projects that were not successful, and situations that required me to find a different approach to solving a certain problem. Difficulties such as these have developed in me resilience and flexibility. Initially, I entered the program in Electrical Engineering to acquire technical skills. However, I will leave this program with a completely different understanding of what being an engineer involves. Not only are technical skills important, but it is equally vital to be an effective communicator, work as part of a team, show ethical responsibility, have an inquisitive mind, and have the willingness to learn continuously. The biggest realization that I have gained from Iowa State is that my engineering education is not complete once I receive my degree.

What is an ethical engineer?

IIntroduction

Engineering is not only about building structures, developing software, or solving technical issues; it is a profession that carries great responsibilities since engineers can shape the world and influence society, the environment, and future generations. That is why ethics plays such an important role in engineering; an ethical engineer is someone who demonstrates ethics, professional responsibility, and personal integrity through their decision-making. By going over the three main ethical theories—consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics—and highlighting the three qualities that characterize an ethical engineer—integrity, responsibility, and commitment—I will examine what it means to be an ethical engineer in this essay.

Engineering has an impact on almost every facet of human life in the modern world. The engineering decisions we make today will affect everything around us, from the streets and roads we walk on to the gadgets and devices we use every day. These choices often pose difficult ethical conundrums for engineers, such as balancing cost with public safety or quality and efficiency with environmental harm. The ever-increasing complexity of today's technological advances, such as artificial intelligence (AI) and global connectivity, further emphasizes the need for ethical engineering standards because these technological advances can have unforeseen negative impacts, and put people, communities, and ecosystems at risk if they are not supported by a solid ethical foundation.

IIEthical Theories and Engineering Practice

Ethical theories provide a solid foundation for understanding and evaluating ethical dilemmas in engineering. Three major ethical theories are consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics, and each of them offers a different perspective on what it means to act ethically.

Consequentialism

According to the philosophy of consequentialism, the morality of an action is determined by the results it produces. This implies that ethical engineers need to think carefully about the implications of their choices and try to maximize the benefits while minimizing the disadvantages. For example, in order to guarantee the highest level of safety and dependability when designing a bridge, a consequentialist engineer would place a higher priority on choosing the best possible materials and the safest construction methods, even if these are more costly, more time-consuming, and more labor-intensive. The primary contention is that moral engineers have an obligation to create choices that are advantageous to all stakeholders. Another instance of consequentialist engineering thought is the development of renewable energy sources. Engineers working with solar and wind power need to weigh the environmental benefits of clean energy against the costs of production and installation. A consequentialist strategy would place long-term viability and low environmental damage above short-term financial gain. However, consequentialism has its own drawbacks. It requires engineers to make accurate predictions, which can often be difficult due to unforeseen factors that can come into the play. Furthermore, unethical practices may be justified if they lead to positive overall outcomes. For example, a company may use less expensive, lower-quality materials in a particular product if it believes that the overall harm is less than the advantages of accessibility and cost savings. Ethical engineers should also be vigilant to prevent unethical shortcuts or reductions in public safety due to their consequential reasoning.

Deontology

Deontology, which is developed by philosopher Immanuel Kant, states that ethics depend not only on consequences but also by doing assigned tasks, following personal obligations, and established rules. According to this point of view, an ethical engineer must do things by the industry standards, professional codes of conduct, and accepted ethical principles, even if doing so would result in dire consequences. For example, although skipping some testing may speed up the manufacturing process, an engineer working on a medical device must ensure that all safety and regulatory requirements are met. Deontological engineers value disciplined action under any circumstances. The aerospace industry's commitment to safety regulations is a practical illustration of deontological ethics in engineering standards. To ensure that aircraft and spacecraft meet strict safety standards, aerospace engineers must adhere to strict regulations set forth by organizations such as NASA and the FAA. If there is a cheaper, faster method of production, engineers must reject it if it violates their safety standards. However, ethics has its problems because it is not perfect. Strict adherence to rules can sometimes make it difficult for engineers to adapt to unexpected situations or come up with more creative solutions. When rules are outdated or do not take into account new technologies, engineers must carefully balance their ethical responsibilities with their drive to innovate and improve.

Virtue Ethics

When comparing to strict rules and high standards or beneficial outcomes, virtue ethics emphasizes a person's moral principles and characteristics more. Ethical engineers who follow this idea strive to develop their personal traits such as honesty, courage, and wisdom. For example, an engineer working on environmental development projects demonstrates virtue stewardship by advocating for environmentally friendly solutions, even if it means going against the company’s best interests. The virtues that engineers need to guide their moral behavior and decision-making can be developed through a process of continuous self-improvement and ethical reflection. Even in situations where regulations lack precise guidance, virtue ethics encourages engineers to develop moral character and behave in a disciplined manner. Even when there is no legal need, an honest engineer will disclose any potential hazards associated with a project. Likewise, a fair-minded engineer will advocate for ethical labor standards and ensure that employees are treated fairly, even in the face of financial hardship.

IIICharacteristics of an Ethical Engineer

Ethical behavior in engineering can take many forms, but three key characteristics define an ethical engineer: integrity, responsibility, and commitment to public benefits. These qualities shape ethical decision-making and help building trust in the engineering profession.

Integrity

For any ethical engineer, integrity is an essential trait. It involves always prioritizing ethical issues before individual or business benefit and acting with transparency and honesty in every element of a profession. Integrity-driven engineers accurately disclose test results and prevent unethical behavior. Consider the Volkswagen emissions scandal, where engineers concealed the true pollution levels while manipulating emissions statistics to comply with standards. An ethical engineer, however, would have upheld integrity by refusing to falsify data, ensuring that public health and the environment came first. Integrity is important because trust is the cornerstone of engineering, and it is built on integrity. Clients, governments, and the public rely on engineers to create safe, functional, and ethical solutions. Without integrity, engineering decisions can be corrupted or negligent, leading to disasters such as structural failure or environmental damage.

Responsibility

In addition to their employers, ethical engineers should be clear about their obligations to society and the environment. In engineering, accountability requires taking responsibility for one’s actions, proactively avoiding harm, and adhering to ethical principles. For example, when constructing a high-rise building, civil engineers must follow safety rules and use durable materials to avoid earthquake threats. If an engineer ignores these responsibilities and approves subpar materials in an attempt to save money, they put lives in danger. The collapse of the Rana Plaza garment factory in Bangladesh in 2013 is an example of what can happen when engineers and business leaders fail to uphold their responsibilities. Engineers should also be tasked with solving ethical puzzles within their companies. When public safety is at risk, it is sometimes necessary to speak up or even report to a whistleblower. To prevent harm, an ethical engineer who discovers dishonest and illegal business practices should take appropriate steps, whether that means filing an internal complaint or going to court.

Commitment to Public Welfare

The public interest must always take precedence over the interests of engineers, their organizations, or their political parties. This means going beyond simply choosing the easiest path and actively seeking answers that minimize harm and improve society. For example, an ethical engineer designing an industrial facility would try to minimize waste by using environmentally friendly alternatives and increasing energy efficiency, even if these options would cost more time, money, and effort. Likewise, biomedical engineers who create medical devices, vaccines, and medicines need to ensure that their creations are not only safe, but also affordable and available to those who need them the most. There is also another emerging field where the public interest is placed on top: artificial intelligence (AI). Engineers who are involved in developing AI, also need to ensure that AI's algorithms not invade public privacy or have negative impacts on society.

IVWhy These Characteristics Matter

The importance of integrity, responsibility, and commitment to public welfare extends beyond individual engineers; these qualities shape the entire profession, benefiting society at large.

  • Trust and Credibility: Integrity fosters public trust in engineering solutions, ensuring that technological advancements are reliable, safe, and beneficial.
  • Safety and Accountability: Responsibility prevents disasters, holds engineers accountable, and promotes a culture of diligence and continuous improvement.
  • Sustainability and Social Justice: Commitment to public welfare ensures that engineering contributes to long-term sustainability, equity, and societal well-being.

These characteristics also resonate with professional engineering codes of ethics, such as the National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) Code of Ethics, which emphasizes honesty, responsibility, and the public interest. Ethical engineers do not merely follow regulations; they actively shape the future of engineering by integrating moral considerations into their daily work.

VConclusion

According to theories like consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics, an ethical engineer is the one who consistently maintains his or her personal traits such as integrity, responsibility, and dedication to the public benefits and interests. In engineering, these qualities are needed to maintain sustainability, safety, and mutual trust among others. The needs for ethical engineers nowadays is growing as engineering problems in general and specifically technology are becoming more complex and advanced. In addition to protecting the public, engineers should also maintain professional integrity and contribute to creating a better world for future generations by adhering to the ethical standards of the profession.
